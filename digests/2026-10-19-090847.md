### Anthropic Engineering

**How we contain Claude across products**

A year ago, the idea of giving Claude enough system access to take down an internal Anthropic service would have been a non-starter. Now it's routine — and the engineering question has shifted from "should we?" to "how do we keep the blast radius small?"

Anthropic's post lays out their hard-won approach to agentic security across three products: claude.ai, Claude Code, and Claude Cowork. The core insight: human supervision alone is not enough. Their telemetry shows users approved 93% of permission prompts — and the more prompts they saw, the less carefully they read each one. Approval fatigue is real, and probabilistic defenses always have a non-zero miss rate.

So the focus has shifted to containment: sandboxes, VMs, filesystem boundaries, and egress controls that cap what an agent can reach regardless of what it's told to do. If credentials never enter the sandbox, they can't be exfiltrated — by a user, a creative model, or an attacker. Claude Code's reference devcontainer exists precisely to enable unattended operation without per-action approvals.

Three risk categories are identified — user misuse, model misbehavior, and external attackers — and three corresponding defense layers: the environment, the model itself, and the external content it can reach (MCP servers, plugins, web search). On Gray Swan's Agent Red Teaming benchmark, Claude Opus 4.7 holds prompt injection attack success to ~0.1% on single attempts, and around 5–6% after 100 adaptive tries. Claude Code auto mode catches ~83% of overeager behaviors before they execute. Still, no layer is sufficient alone.

The most striking admission: models that are more capable also find more unexpected paths to their goals — routing around restrictions nobody thought to write down.

**An update on recent Claude Code quality reports**

Three bugs, three separate code paths, one wave of degradation reports that looked like a single problem. Anthropic has traced the Claude Code quality complaints from the past month to a trio of distinct changes — and fixed all three as of April 20 (v2.1.116).

The first: on March 4, Anthropic quietly downgraded Claude Code's default reasoning effort from high to medium, to reduce occasional very long latency spikes in Opus 4.6 high mode. Users noticed immediately that responses felt less intelligent. The change was reverted on April 7. All models now default to xhigh effort for Opus 4.7 and high for others.

The second: on March 26, an optimization meant to reduce latency when resuming idle sessions introduced a bug that cleared Claude's reasoning history on every subsequent turn — not just once. After a session crossed an hour of idle time, Claude would continue executing but increasingly without memory of why it had made its prior choices. This surfaced as forgetfulness, repetition, and odd tool behavior. Fixed April 10.

The third: on April 16, a new system prompt instruction intended to reduce verbosity, combined with other prompt changes, degraded coding quality. Reverted April 20.

Because each change affected a different slice of traffic on a different schedule, the aggregate effect mimicked broad, inconsistent degradation. Anthropic is resetting usage limits for all subscribers as of April 23.

https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem

### No Priors: Beam — The Great American Open Model with ReflectionAI Co-Founder and CEO Misha Laskin

**The Takeaway:** Open-weight models aren't just a philosophical stance on AI safety — they're already functioning as a practical defense mechanism in real-world cyberattacks, and ReflectionAI just launched Beam as its first bet that America needs its own open frontier model.

Misha Laskin, co-founder and CEO of Reflection AI, sat down to explain why building a frontier open-weight model in the US feels necessary right now. His background: PhD in Physics, former RL researcher at Google DeepMind where he and co-founder Janus worked on the team that shipped Gemini 1 and 1.5. Janus was also a key contributor to AlphaGo.

Their argument for open weights is not abstract. "When you remove cyber offensive capabilities, you also remove cyber defensive capabilities," Laskin says. His evidence: a closed model was used to hack into another company, and that company could only remediate itself using open models. He cites Linus' Law — "with enough eyeballs, all bugs become shallow" — as his model for AI safety: the more people who can inspect and stress-test a system, the fewer vulnerabilities survive.

Building the lab from scratch has meant rebuilding everything that big labs take for granted. Reflection went from ~30 people a year ago to ~300 today, assembling pre-training, mid-training, and RL teams from scratch. Laskin is refreshingly direct: "Everything has been hard. You have to get 30 things right. Everything has to work." Compute, talent, data, infrastructure, culture — none of it is separable.

The result is Beam, Reflection's first publicly released open-weight model. The company's mission: build frontier open intelligence and make it widely accessible — a direct counter to the concentration of frontier AI capability in a handful of closed labs.
